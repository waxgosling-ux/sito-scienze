Abstract

The Reality of the Sacred Mountain

To fully understand Mount Fuji, one must go beyond Shinto phenomenology and the classical representation of ukiyo-e prints, which postulate the mountain as a sacred and immutable monolith. The reality is that of a complex thermodynamic machine: an active stratovolcano subject to strong tectonic pressures, entropic geological decay, and today, the wear derived from the ecological collapse of mass tourism.

Section 01 — Earth Sciences

Mathematical, Mereotopological and Energetic Analysis

Morphogenesis

Fuji exhibits a precise symmetry given by a manifestation of differential equations inherent in the physics of granular materials. It is characterized by a nearly perfect conical geometry, maintained by the balance between the gravitational gradient and the angle of repose invariant typical of pyroclastic deposition.

Forces acting on a pyroclastic mass on the volcanic slope: gravitational component (Fg) balanced by friction limit (Ff) at the angle of repose θ.

Mereotopology

To evaluate the actual extent of the system, it is necessary to apply the logic of part-whole relationships. There is a deep topological and hydrogeological continuity between the main volcanic vent and the peripheral entities. This indissolubly links the Five Lakes of Fuji and the Shiraito Falls, making them a single ecosystem.

Section 02 — Sociology & Economics

Sociology and Economy

Institutional Timeline

Heian & Kamakura Periods
Establishment of Shugendo ascetics and early forms of structured veneration and pilgrimage.
Tokugawa Shogunate (1707)
The Hoei Eruption. Catastrophic ash deposition precipitating severe macroeconomic deflation and agricultural disruption across the Kanto plain.
Meiji Restoration
Removal of religious prohibitions: everyone is allowed to climb the mountain regardless of social status or gender (end of the ban for women).
Contemporary Era
Hyper-commodification and transition from sacred site to internationally contested global tourist resource.
"Mountains function as inadequately regulated commons — their carrying capacity is not infinite, and the debt is paid in ecological collapse."

Overtourism and Collapse

Mountains function as inadequately regulated commons. Drawing on Elinor Ostrom's economic models, current overtourism leads to an unsustainable exploitation of the environment's carrying capacity, threatening the ecological collapse of trails and severe pollution.

Governance Solutions

Access Fees

Introduce fair rules to charge those who pollute and consume resources during overcrowding, covering environmental damage and maintenance costs.

Reservation Topologies

Using strict digital limits and closed-number systems to prevent system overloads during periods of highest tourist demand.

Allocation Algorithms

Dynamic redistribution of human flows across the four primary trails to equalize ecological degradation and avoid bottlenecks.

Section 03 — Conclusions & Sources

Project Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the analysis of a complex ecosystem like Mount Fuji necessarily requires a multidisciplinary approach. Limiting oneself to the aesthetic or literary aspect ignores the thermodynamic reality of the mountain, while a purely geological view fails to account for the anthropogenic factor and the damage derived from the current economic model. The true challenge for the future is the implementation of governance algorithms that balance human use with the physical conservation of the volcano.
